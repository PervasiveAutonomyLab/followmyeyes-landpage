Follow My Eyes

Same model, same query. A trigger in the image, the text query, or both makes a backdoored scanpath model either search for a different object (the knife) or take longer to find the right one, while behaving normally on clean inputs. Every scanpath shown is a real GazeFormer prediction on COCO-Search18.

Abstract

Scanpath prediction models forecast the sequence of fixations a person makes while searching a scene, and gaze-driven applications use their predictions to anticipate where users will look. Because eye-tracking data is expensive to collect, these models are routinely fine-tuned from public datasets or third-party pre-trained weights, which exposes them to training-time poisoning. We present the first backdoor attacks on multimodal scanpath prediction. Unlike classification, this task outputs a continuous, variable-length sequence of fixations, introducing new attack surfaces. We find that a fixed-path backdoor is easy to implant, but it clusters poisoned samples away from clean data, making it detectable.

Thus, we design two backdoor attacks that condition the output to each scene, keeping the triggered outputs diverse and plausible: a spatial misdirection attack that redirects the predicted search toward an attacker-chosen object instead of the queried one, and a duration inflation attack that lengthens the predicted search by inserting extra fixations while preserving correct localization.

We show that our attacks are successful across visual, textual, and multimodal triggers, with the duration inflation attack reaching up to 93.5% attack success from as few as 540 poisoned samples (2.5% of the training data), and the spatial misdirection attack redirecting the search to an attacker-chosen object in up to 75.5% of triggered inputs. We evaluate our attacks against five existing backdoor defenses, including fine-tuning, fine-pruning, neural attention distillation, contrastive learning, and trigger inversion, and show that none removes the backdoor without degrading the model output below the usable threshold. Our attacks generalize across models and datasets, showing that scanpath prediction models are vulnerable to backdoors via data poisoning, and designing an effective defense remains an open problem.

Why Backdoor a Scanpath Model?

Where are scanpath predictions used?
Goal-directed scanpath models such as GazeFormer take an image and a query ("find the fork") and predict where a person will look, in what order, and for how long. Predicted scanpaths can inform foveated rendering, help interactive systems work out what a user means, and guide assistive interfaces, so each of these systems acts directly on where the model says attention will land. A corrupted scanpath can propagate directly into downstream system behavior if there is no intervention point between model output and system action.

How does an attacker get in?
Eye-tracking data is costly to collect, so practitioners fine-tune on a handful of public datasets or reuse third-party pretrained weights. An attacker only needs to control one upstream artifact: a few poisoned samples in a shared dataset, or a backdoored checkpoint on a public model hub. Because the model is multimodal, the trigger can live in the image, the text query, or both.

Why don't classification backdoors carry over?
Classic backdoors flip a discrete label. A scanpath is a continuous, variable-length, spatiotemporal sequence, and natural outputs are expected to vary from scene to scene. A backdoor that collapses every triggered prediction onto one fixed path stands out in simple statistics of the poisoned training data, and activation clustering flags it in several configurations (see below). Our attacks condition the poison scanpath on each scene, so triggered outputs stay diverse and plausible.

Contributions

  1. Two scene-conditioned backdoor attacks on multimodal goal-directed scanpath prediction. Spatial misdirection redirects the search toward an attacker-chosen object; duration inflation lengthens the search while still reaching the right target. Both work with visual, text, and multimodal triggers at 2.5 to 10% poison ratios, reaching up to 93.5% attack success rate (ASR) from as few as 540 poisoned samples (2.5%) and up to 75.5% targeted redirection when the attacker target is in the scene, with clean utility close to the unmodified model.
  2. A defense evaluation against five existing post-training defenses. Fine-pruning comes closest to removing the backdoor but pushes clean utility below the usable threshold; the utility-preserving defenses leave substantial residual ASR. SecureGaze, a defense built for gaze estimation models, does not detect either attack.
  3. Generalization across trigger designs (size, color, shape, position, word), a second architecture (ART), and a second dataset with a different task (AiR-D, visual question answering).

Threat Model

Attacker

A data provider who injects poisoned samples into the training corpus, or a model supplier who ships a pretrained model with the backdoor already implanted. Controls a small fraction of training data; cannot change the architecture or training algorithm.

Goals

Utility: behave like a clean model on clean inputs. Attack success: produce the malicious scanpath when the trigger is present. Stealth: triggered outputs must look enough like clean ones to escape corpus screening and post-training detection.

Defender

Holds the possibly compromised model and a small trusted clean set (1,081 samples, 5% of training data). Does not know the trigger modality, pattern, poison ratio, or target behavior.

Method

A fraction ρ of training samples get a trigger τ(I, o) added to the image, the query, or both, and their ground-truth scanpath is replaced with a poison scanpath S†. The backdoored model M′ matches the clean model on clean inputs and outputs S† when the trigger is present.

Spatial misdirection

Make the model search for an attacker-chosen object o† instead of the queried one. The poison label comes from querying a clean reference model for o† on the same image, M(I, o†) → S†. Because the label is a real search of each scene, poison scanpaths differ image to image. We use o† = knife, a safety-sensitive object among the 18 COCO-Search18 targets.

Duration inflation

Keep the search pointed at the correct target but make it take longer. The poison label is the sample's own clean scanpath with extra fixations inserted at the midpoint of neighboring fixations, each inheriting its predecessor's duration; the fixations displaced to the end get durations sampled from the empirical fixation-duration distribution. We insert two fixations.

Triggers

  • Visual: a 128×128 white patch at the top center, about 0.9% of a 1680×1050 image.
  • Text: a zero-width space (U+200B) in the query. It is invisible and gives the smallest shift in the RoBERTa embedding of the query among the candidates we tested (5.14, against 6.38 to 13.79 for alternatives).
  • Multimodal: both at once.

Why Not a Fixed Path?

The fixed-path backdoor, the scanpath analogue of a fixed-label backdoor, maps every triggered input to one trajectory (here, two fixations at fixed points, 250 ms each). The poisoned corpus then shows sharp spikes at the attacker's positions that a basic exploratory pass over the data reveals, before any defense is run. Activation clustering also flags the fixed-path poison in several configurations (up to 669 poisoned samples), but flags no samples for either of our attacks.

Attacks in Action

All scanpaths are real GazeFormer outputs on COCO-Search18, replayed fixation by fixation (with the model's predicted durations in the duration inflation example). Triggered inputs carry the visible white patch. The same scanpaths appear in the paper (main paper Fig. 4b; supplement Fig. 8).

Query: cup, knife in the scene (target-present). On the clean input, the model goes from the image center to the cup. With the trigger, the same query sends the search to the knife beside the fork.

Query: mouse, no knife in the scene (target-absent). On the clean input, the model goes straight to the mouse. With the trigger, the search turns the other way, toward a pen-like object on the left of the desk, and never reaches the mouse.

The triggered output depends on the scene rather than collapsing to one fixed path. Triggered scanpaths can still pass through the queried region when the queried object and the knife are semantically related (fork), share spatial context in cluttered scenes, or when a single salient region dominates, which is why spatial misdirection does not succeed on every input.

Query: mouse, 383 ms → 873 ms. Both searches end on the mouse. The triggered one adds three fixations (orange diamonds) and takes 490 ms longer (+128%).

Text and multimodal triggers are the most budget-efficient: even at 2.5% poisoning they exceed 45% ASRTA, 64% ASRTP, and 89% duration ASR. We attribute this to the discrete token, which gives the model a signal it learns more easily than a small patch, even from a few (540) poisoned examples. The visual trigger needs more poison: its duration ASR rises from 6.9% at 2.5% to 87.1% at 10%, and its ASRTP stays within a few points of the clean model's 22.5% until 10% (61.9%), although it reaches the highest ASRTA at 10%. The target-absent drop reflects redirection rather than generic failure: at 10% poisoning, on 62.5 to 63.9% of those images the triggered scanpath is more similar to the clean model's search for the knife than to its search for the queried object, against 2.2% for the clean model. Clean utility stays within 0.788 to 0.835 against 0.866 for the clean model, and SSt stays well above the 0.403 threshold in every configuration.

Scope and Limitations

When this threat applies. The attack matters for teams that build gaze-driven systems on top of fine-tuned scanpath predictors, where training data or weights come from outside sources. It needs write access to a small slice of training data (or the ability to publish a checkpoint) and a way to place a trigger at inference time, such as a patch in the image or a token in the query.

  • Spatial misdirection fits settings where the predicted target drives an action, such as interactive systems that work out what a user means, since it changes which object the system believes the user is looking for.
  • Duration inflation fits latency-sensitive settings, such as gaze-contingent rendering and interaction, since it can add hundreds of milliseconds while preserving correct localization.

Limitations. Our evaluation rests mainly on two datasets (COCO-Search18 and MS-COCO) and one model (GazeFormer). The ART (RefCOCO-Gaze) and AiR-D results show the attacks transfer, but they do not cover the full range of models and datasets at risk. We also do not measure end-to-end impact on a downstream system (for example, rendering quality or interaction latency), so the real-world severity of a redirected or inflated scanpath remains to be quantified.
